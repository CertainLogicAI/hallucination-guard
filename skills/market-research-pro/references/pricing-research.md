# Pricing Research Frameworks

## Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter

Ask 4 questions to find the acceptable price range:

1. At what price would you consider [product] to be **so cheap** you'd question its quality?
2. At what price would you consider [product] a **bargain** — a great buy for the money?
3. At what price would [product] start to seem **expensive** — you'd have to think about it?
4. At what price would [product] be **too expensive** — you'd never consider buying it?

### How to Analyze
Plot the cumulative distribution of answers on a chart:
- **Point of Marginal Cheapness (PMC):** "Too cheap" intersects "Expensive" → below this, people doubt quality
- **Point of Marginal Expensiveness (PME):** "Too expensive" intersects "Bargain" → above this, you lose most buyers
- **Optimal Price Point (OPP):** "Too cheap" intersects "Too expensive" → least resistance
- **Indifference Price Point (IDP):** "Bargain" intersects "Expensive" → equal split

**Your pricing range:** PMC to PME. Your sweet spot: OPP to IDP.

**Minimum sample:** 30 respondents from your target segment. More = tighter ranges.

## Gabor-Granger Method

Simpler. Test specific price points to find max revenue.

### Process
1. Pick 5-7 price points spanning your expected range
2. Ask each respondent: "Would you buy [product] at $X?" (yes/no)
3. Start at the middle price, then go up or down based on answer
4. Calculate demand curve: % who said yes at each price
5. Revenue = Price × % who'd buy at that price
6. Pick the price that maximizes revenue

### Example
| Price | % Would Buy | Revenue Index |
|-------|-------------|---------------|
| $9 | 80% | 720 |
| $15 | 65% | 975 |
| $19 | 55% | **1045** ← winner |
| $25 | 35% | 875 |
| $35 | 15% | 525 |

## Competitive Pricing Analysis

### Price Positioning Matrix

Map competitors on two axes:
- **X-axis:** Price (low → high)
- **Y-axis:** Value/features (low → high)

| Quadrant | Position | Strategy |
|----------|----------|----------|
| High value, low price | **Penetration** | Win on value, risk margin squeeze |
| High value, high price | **Premium** | Justify with brand/quality/results |
| Low value, low price | **Economy** | Volume play, race to bottom |
| Low value, high price | **Overpriced** | Vulnerable to disruption |

### What to Capture Per Competitor
- List price (monthly / annual / one-time)
- Discount patterns (annual billing, startup discounts, volume)
- Free tier limits
- Feature differences at each tier
- Hidden costs (setup, support, integrations, overages)

## Pricing Psychology

### Anchoring
- Show the most expensive option first → makes mid-tier feel reasonable
- "Was $X, now $Y" only works if X is believable
- Enterprise tier existence makes Pro tier feel accessible

### Tiering Rules
- **3 tiers** is optimal (paradox of choice with 5+)
- **Middle tier** should be the one you want most people to buy
- Name tiers by outcome, not features ("Starter → Growth → Scale")
- Each tier should have one clear reason to upgrade

### Number Psychology
- $19 > $20 (left-digit effect)
- $97 > $99 (perceived deliberate pricing vs rounded laziness)
- $X/day feels smaller than $X/month ("less than a coffee")
- Annual pricing should offer 15-20% discount over monthly (enough to nudge, not so much it devalues)

## Willingness to Pay Signals

Evidence stronger than survey responses:

| Signal | Strength | How to Find |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| Pre-orders / deposits | 🟢 Strongest | Offer it and see |
| Letter of intent (LOI) | 🟢 Strong | Ask enterprise prospects |
| Waitlist with payment info | 🟢 Strong | Landing page test |
| Current spend on alternatives | 🟡 Good | Customer interviews |
| Price comparison shopping | 🟡 Good | Sales conversations |
| Survey responses | 🔴 Weakest | Van Westendorp/Gabor-Granger |

**Rule:** Revealed preference (what they do) > Stated preference (what they say).
